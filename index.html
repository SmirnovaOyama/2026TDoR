Say their names

Transgender Day of Remembrance

Every year on 20 November, people light candles and read aloud the names of trans people who were killed in the past twelve months. The reading goes slowly on purpose. Each name was someone with a home, friends, a way of walking into a room. Each light on this page stands for one of them.

Who they were

The killings do not fall evenly. They land hardest on people already pushed to the edge of protection: trans women, people of colour, sex workers, migrants. Seven in ten happened in Latin America and the Caribbean.1

      The ones the count misses

      A death enters the count only if someone reports it, and only if someone knows the person was trans.

      In the United States, coroners' reports and death certificates do not record whether a person was trans.2 After a death, some families never say so. Some people are buried under a name they had stopped using, and the record goes with them.

      This year's total is under 300 for only the second time in a decade. TGEU does not read that as fewer killings. Shrinking civic space, hostile media and governments, and cuts to funding make a death less likely to be reported and recorded.1

      What a name can do

      The same word keeps the living

      For trans young people, each more place where they could use their chosen name went with 29% fewer suicidal thoughts and 56% less suicidal behaviour.3

      A study of 129 trans and gender-nonconforming young people in three US cities, asking about home, school, work and friends

      The vigil is built around names because a name is where recognition starts. Research on the living keeps finding the same thing. Being seen as who you are protects people, and it costs very little to give.

      73%

      Lower odds of suicidal thoughts or self-harm among young people who started puberty blockers or hormones, over about a year of follow-up at a Seattle clinic4

      34%

      Lower odds of a suicide attempt in the past year for trans and nonbinary young people of colour with high support from family5

      These studies show associations and cannot prove cause. Their samples are small or self-selected. They still point the same way.

      We read the names of the dead once a year. We can say the names of the living every day.

      From one vigil to many

      How it began

      1. 28 November 1998

        Rita Hester, a Black trans woman, is killed in her apartment in Allston, Massachusetts. Newspapers report her death under a name she no longer used and call her a man. Her murder has never been solved.6 8

      2. February 1999

        Gwendolyn Ann Smith starts Remembering Our Dead, a website listing trans people lost to violence. She bases its design on the Vietnam Veterans Memorial, a wall of names.6

      3. November 1999

        About a hundred people hold a candlelight vigil in San Francisco and read the names aloud. It becomes the first Transgender Day of Remembrance.6

      4. 2009

        TGEU starts the Trans Murder Monitoring project, with records reaching back to 2008.7

      5. 2026

        Vigils take place in cities across the world. The count reaches 5,592.7

      Find a vigil

      Vigils are held on and around 20 November. Local trans groups, LGBTQ+ centres and Pride organisations usually list them. TGEU gathers the year's materials and the full monitoring data.
